## Background

Health AI models are increasingly deployed in clinical settings, yet evaluation remains fragmented, decontextualized, and disconnected from improvement. Current fairness auditing relies on demographic categories (race, sex) that are social constructs, imperfectly collected in EHR data, and insufficient to capture the structural biases embedded in care delivery. Our preliminary work demonstrates the limitations of this approach: we trained a mortality prediction model on MIMIC-IV (AUC 0.89) that showed no significant performance variation across race or sex — yet when we stratified by *care phenotypes* (patient subgroups defined by treatment patterns such as monitoring frequency, adjusted for illness severity via APACHE-IV), we identified a subgroup with a 12-percentage-point drop in AUC. Standard demographic auditing declared the model fair; care phenotype auditing revealed hidden failure. Separately, shortcut learning — where imaging models exploit spurious correlations like chest tube presence rather than pathology — remains a persistent and under-detected failure mode, with recent studies showing 30-40% of published models are affected (Degrave et al., Nature Machine Intelligence, 2021).

Meanwhile, the expertise to build, evaluate, and improve health AI is concentrated in a small number of institutions. When failures are identified, the path from diagnosis to remedy is unclear — there is no infrastructure connecting evaluation findings to actionable improvement resources. Models get scored and abandoned, not coached and improved.

## What Is Dojo?

We propose **Dojo**, a platform where health AI models are evaluated across multiple dimensions and then provided with concrete, actionable improvement pathways — tools, data, and expert guidance. Dojo is built on two technical innovations (shortcut detection and care phenotype-based fairness auditing) and one structural innovation (closing the gap between evaluation and improvement through a coached feedback loop).

Dojo operationalizes the LTARC framework: evaluations are **Local** (contextualized to deployment setting and patient population), **Task-specific** (tailored to the clinical task rather than generic benchmarks), **Agile** (adapting to model updates and shifting deployment contexts), **Reflexive** (Dojo's own agents are subject to the same evaluation), and **Community-powered** (governed by a multi-stakeholder body, not any single institution).

## Specific Aims

### Aim 1: Develop and validate two core evaluation agents for health AI auditing

**Aim 1a — Shortcut Detective Agent.** We will build an automated agent that, given a medical imaging model and its training/test data, identifies reliance on spurious correlations (shortcut features) for classification. The agent will apply a three-stage pipeline: (1) feature attribution mapping using integrated gradients and GradCAM to identify high-influence image regions; (2) spurious correlation testing via subgroup ablation — systematically masking candidate shortcut features (e.g., imaging artifacts, demographic markers, device-specific patterns) and measuring prediction shift; (3) counterfactual generation using diffusion-based image editing to confirm causality. Output: a structured shortcut report listing identified spurious features, affected subgroups, estimated performance impact of each shortcut, and specific remediation recommendations (data augmentation strategies, feature masking during training, architecture modifications). **Validation:** We will benchmark against 12 known shortcut cases from the literature (chest X-ray, dermatology, retinal imaging) and measure detection sensitivity and false positive rate. Target: >85% sensitivity at <15% FPR.

**Aim 1b — Care Phenotype Agent.** We will build an agent that generates treatment-pattern-based patient subgroups (care phenotypes) from clinical data and uses them for fairness auditing as an alternative — or complement — to demographic categories. The agent will: (1) extract care process variables from EHR data (monitoring frequency, intervention timing, medication administration patterns, documentation frequency); (2) adjust for illness severity and clinical confounders using propensity score weighting (APACHE-IV for ICU, Charlson for general ward); (3) cluster patients using Gaussian mixture models on the adjusted care process features; (4) audit model performance across resulting care phenotypes and compare disparities found vs. those found with standard demographic stratification. Output: care phenotype definitions with clinical interpretability scores, a comparative fairness report (phenotype-based vs. demographic-based), and identification of "hidden disparity" subgroups. **Validation:** We will apply the agent to 3 clinical prediction tasks (mortality, sepsis onset, readmission) across 2 datasets (MIMIC-IV, eICU) and measure: (a) whether care phenotypes reveal performance disparities invisible to demographic auditing (hypothesis: yes, based on preliminary data), and (b) inter-dataset stability of care phenotype definitions (hypothesis: phenotypes will be partially dataset-specific, reflecting local care practices — this is a feature, not a bug, consistent with the LTARC Local principle).

### Aim 2: Design a coached improvement loop that closes the evaluation-to-remediation gap

Current evaluation stops at diagnosis. Dojo's coached improvement loop provides concrete remediation after each evaluation finding. We will design and implement three coaching interventions:

**Intervention 1 — Automated Remediation Plans.** For each evaluation finding (shortcut detected, care phenotype disparity identified), the system generates a structured remediation plan: specific retraining strategies, data augmentation approaches, architecture modifications, or deployment constraints. These plans are generated using a retrieval-augmented system over a curated knowledge base of published remediation strategies, indexed by failure type and clinical domain.

**Intervention 2 — Curated Remediation Resources.** A versioned, community-maintained library of debiased training datasets, augmentation pipelines, shortcut-mitigating preprocessing tools, and fairness-constrained training recipes. Each resource is tagged with applicable failure types, clinical domains, and empirical evidence of effectiveness.

**Intervention 3 — Structured Expert Matching.** A lightweight matching system connecting teams with community members who have solved similar problems, based on failure type, clinical domain, and data modality. Matches are facilitated through structured consultation templates (not open-ended mentorship), with documented outcomes feeding back into the knowledge base.

**Validation:** We will recruit 8-12 teams (academic labs, health systems, industry) to submit models through the full Dojo pipeline (evaluation → coaching → re-evaluation). Primary outcome: proportion of teams that achieve statistically significant improvement on identified weaknesses upon re-evaluation (target: >60%). Secondary outcomes: time from evaluation to remediation, team satisfaction, knowledge base growth rate.

### Aim 3: Establish multi-stakeholder governance and evaluate community impact

We will co-design Dojo's governance using Value Sensitive Design (VSD) methodology with four stakeholder groups: (1) clinical AI developers (academic and industry), (2) clinicians who use AI tools, (3) patient advocacy groups and community organizations, and (4) health equity and ethics researchers. The co-design process will follow Friedman & Hendry's tripartite VSD framework: conceptual investigation (stakeholder and value identification), empirical investigation (stakeholder interviews and surveys, N≥30), and technical investigation (translating values into platform design requirements).

**Governance deliverables:** (1) A decision-making charter specifying roles, voting mechanisms, and conflict resolution processes for platform decisions (agent inclusion/removal, data policies, resource allocation); (2) a data governance protocol covering model/data submission terms, privacy protections (sandboxed execution, no model weight extraction, HIPAA-compliant data handling), retention policies, and intellectual property agreements; (3) an agent validation protocol specifying that every Dojo agent undergoes review by ≥3 human reviewers from ≥2 stakeholder groups, with inter-rater reliability (Fleiss' kappa >0.6) required before deployment.

**Community impact evaluation:** We hypothesize that structured engagement with AI evaluation and remediation shifts participant practices and values (we term this "AI plasticity"). We will measure this through pre/post surveys (adapted from the Responsible Innovation Assessment framework) administered to all Dojo participants, assessing changes in: fairness consideration in model development, stakeholder engagement practices, and environmental awareness. This is framed as an exploratory research question, not a foundational assumption.

## Innovation

1. **Care phenotypes as fairness audit categories.** First platform to operationalize treatment-pattern-based subgrouping as a systematic alternative to demographic stratification for AI fairness auditing, with empirical evidence that it reveals hidden disparities.
2. **Coached evaluation.** First platform to pair AI evaluation with structured, evidence-based remediation — automated plans, curated resources, and expert matching — with measurable improvement as the success metric, not just score reporting.
3. **Reflexive validation.** Dojo's own agents are subject to the same multi-stakeholder review and adversarial testing as submitted models, avoiding the "who watches the watchers" problem through explicit human-in-the-loop validation protocols.

## Impact

If successful, Dojo will demonstrate that health AI evaluation can be (a) multi-dimensional, capturing failure modes invisible to standard demographic auditing; (b) actionable, with coached improvement pathways that measurably improve submitted models; and (c) community-governed, with transparent processes designed by diverse stakeholders. The platform, agents, remediation knowledge base, and governance framework will be open-source, enabling replication and adaptation by any institution. All grant-writing and co-design processes will be recorded and published as open educational resources.
